I combine seismic observations, satellite geodesy, and source modeling to connect what we observe at the surface with what happens on a fault.

SCIENCE2025 · MANDALAY, MYANMAR

What keeps a
rupture supershear?

The 2025 Mandalay earthquake offers a window into sustained supershear rupture. Seismic imaging and geodetic observations reveal how contrasting materials across a fault and a favorable energy balance helped maintain exceptionally fast rupture.

SCIENCE2024 · NOTO, JAPAN

One asperity.
Two rupture initiations.

A strong fault patch resisted earlier swarms and slowed the initial rupture. A second initiation on the other side enabled rupture fronts to encircle the asperity—revealing how a barrier can shape a major earthquake.

Explore the study ↗
JGR: SOLID EARTH2025 · METHODS

Looking through
the Earth’s core.

Most large seismic arrays are in the Northern Hemisphere. PKIKP back-projection uses waves that travel through Earth’s core to extend rupture imaging to earthquakes beyond the reach of conventional P-wave observations.

Explore the method ↗

NATURE GEOSCIENCE · 2022

Supershear is
more widespread
than we thought.

A systematic global search revealed supershear rupture in at least 14% of the large, shallow strike-slip earthquakes studied from 2000–2020, with oceanic events occurring as frequently as continental ones.

MORE CASE STUDIES
2025Unzipping a conjugate fault systemHualien, Taiwan, China · GRL＋

The 2024 Mw 7.4 Hualien earthquake illuminates how rupture unfolds across a conjugate fault system. Combining complementary observations helps resolve fault geometry and the sequence of fault activation.

Read the paper ↗
2023A bilateral rupture on bifurcating faultsMaduo, China · JGR＋

Seismic and geodetic observations resolve a bilateral rupture along a roughly 160 km fault system in the 2021 Mw 7.4 Maduo earthquake, including branching near its eastern end.

Read the paper ↗
2023Following rupture across fault segmentsTürkiye · CEE＋

The 2023 Mw 7.8 Kahramanmaraş earthquake ruptured multiple fault segments. Joint observations reveal its overall subshear propagation and the complexity of rupture across a large continental fault network.

03 / About me

Curious about
a moving Earth.

I’m a postdoctoral scholar at the California Institute of Technology, working in the Seismological Laboratory and the Division of Geological and Planetary Sciences.

My research spans continental faults, subduction zones, and oceanic transform boundaries. I use seismic and geodetic data to study earthquake source processes, and connect observations with modeling to understand the mechanics of rupture.

I received my PhD from UCLA and my BS from Zhejiang University. I’m especially interested in supershear earthquakes, the role of fault asperities, and new approaches to global source imaging.
